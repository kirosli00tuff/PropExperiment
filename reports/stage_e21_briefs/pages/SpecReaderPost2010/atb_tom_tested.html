The Turn-of-the-Month Effect, Tested on Current Data

Three markets, current data, and a widely cited anomaly that mostly doesn't survive the test.

Pablo Trindade
Jul 27, 2026

Carchano and Pardo's 2011 study of S&P 500, DAX, and Nikkei 225 futures tested 188 calendar anomalies. Only one held up as both statistically significant and economically persistent: the turn-of-the-month (TOM) effect. TOM is defined simply, the last trading day of the month plus the first three trading days of the next, four days out of roughly twenty-one.

That paper’s data runs from 1991 to 2008. It’s 2026 now, so we pulled real 60-minute futures data for three markets, the S&P 500 E-mini (ES, back to 1997), Gold (GC, back to 2002), and the 30-Year T-Bond (US, back to 2001), and ran them through the same TOM/non-TOM split using our own bias-dashboard calculators rather than taking the original paper’s word for it.

The headline number: it doesn't hold up broadly

For each market we split every trading day into TOM (last day of month plus first 3 trading days) or non-TOM, and compared average daily P&L.

None of these results are statistically significant. Every p-value sits well above 0.05. Worse, in ES and the bond, the TOM window actually underperforms the rest of the month. Only Gold points in the direction Carchano and Pardo predicted, and even there the effect isn’t distinguishable from noise.

That’s not what a persistent, robust calendar effect is supposed to look like twenty years later.

It gets worse in the recent decade

Splitting each market's full history in half, and separately looking at just the trailing ten years, sharpens the story.

ES’s TOM edge has decayed to essentially zero and gone negative in the most recent decade, while the rest of the month has been strongly positive, the opposite of what a “buy TOM, hold cash otherwise” strategy would want. Gold is the one market where the TOM tailwind actually grew over time. Bonds flipped sign entirely between the two halves.

This is exactly the pattern the Adaptive Market Hypothesis predicts: anomalies that get discovered, published, and arbitraged tend to fade or invert, and the fade isn’t uniform across markets.

Williams' sharper, conditional version tells a different story

The broad four-day TOM window may just be too blunt an instrument. Larry Williams' approach, filtering a specific trading-day-of-month by a specific day of the week, is a narrower, higher-conviction bet. We replicated three of his published setups against current data (naive 1-day hold, no protective stop, see the caveat below).

Two things stand out.

First, the ES conditional rules are the standout. Sharpe ratios of 2.4 to 3.9 on a simple one-day hold, with n in the 50s, is a genuinely strong signal-to-noise ratio for a calendar-only filter.

Second, Gold’s Tuesday TDOM-6 rule has stopped working. The book’s version of this trade reports a win rate near 77% over decades of data. On the full 2002 to 2026 dataset it’s below a coin flip and net negative. Whatever edge existed there looks arbitraged away, front-run, or specific to the sample window it was originally tested on.

Caveat on the comparison. The published win rates for these setups (77 to 96% in the original research) come from backtests using a protective stop and an “exit on first profitable opening” rule, over specific historical windows. Our replication uses a flat one-day hold with no stop, over each market’s entire available history. The gap between our numbers and the book’s isn’t necessarily decay, some of it is methodology. Before trading any of this, the honest next step is to rebuild the exact exit logic and confirm the divergence is really about time and not about rules.

Caveat on sample count. Five setups tested, one of them negative, is a small enough set that we should be cautious about calling the four winners a pattern rather than a subset that happened to work. The next useful step isn’t finding more Williams setups to test, it’s running the same TDOM-by-day-of-week scan unconditionally across all 44 markets in the dashboard and seeing how many survive without being cherry-picked from a published book first.

What this means if you trade calendar effects

  • Don’t trust the average. The broad TOM window is not a free lunch in any of these three markets today. It wasn’t significant in any of them, and it’s been negative in ES’s most recent decade.

  • Specificity beats breadth. Conditioning on day-of-week within a trading-day-of-month narrows the sample but sharpens the edge, at least in ES and bonds.

  • Check for decay before you size a position. Gold’s Tuesday effect looked identical to the ES and bond setups in a research paper. It isn’t identical in live data. The only way to know is to split the history and look, not to assume persistence.

  • A calendar effect is a starting hypothesis, not a signal. Pair it with a stability check (do both halves of history agree) and a look at whether your exit rule, not just your entry day, is where the edge actually lives.

The sizing test confirms it. Run half-Kelly sizing on the raw TOM window across all three markets and it comes back at zero. Not small, zero. The math is telling you the same thing the p-values are: the bar-level edge on the broad TOM window isn't strong enough to put money behind, in any of these three markets, full stop. The conditional TDOM-plus-day-of-week setups are the only place in this post where a position size above zero is actually defensible.
