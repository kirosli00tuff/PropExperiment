### E1-ML-01. Mesfin (2026), MNQ LSTM vs gradient boosting
- Citation: Mathias Mesfin, "Sequential Structure in Intraday Futures Data: LSTM vs Gradient Boosting on MNQ", arXiv:2605.17724 (May 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17724
- Retrieved: full text, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17724 (curl + pdftotext).
- Claim: neither HistGradientBoosting nor a small LSTM predicts MNQ rest-of-session direction out of sample. The author attributes this to insufficient data.
- Quotes: "No configuration produces out-of-sample accuracy materially above base rate. Combined out-of-sample accuracies range from 50.00% to 50.89% across all gradient boosting variants. The LSTM produces combined OOS accuracy of 50.59%. Permutation test p-values are 0.135 for the best gradient boosting configuration and 0.515 for the LSTM—both failing the p < 0.05 significance threshold." / "Feature importance analysis across walk-forward folds reveals systematic instability: the features dominating each fold differ substantially, confirming noise fitting rather than consistent structural signal capture." / "the sample size required for transformer-scale architectures to learn non-trivial financial patterns is orders of magnitude larger than what is available in four years of single-instrument intraday data." / Data: "The primary dataset consists of 72,604 five-minute OHLCV bars for MNQ continuous front-month futures covering regular trading hours (09:30–16:00 ET) from December 2021 through September 2025."
- Market: MNQ (CME Micro E-mini Nasdaq-100), RTH only. Horizon: 10:30 ET to session close (about 5.5 h); the daily variant is open to close. Data: 944 usable days, one instrument, 29 to 30 features. Cost treatment: none (classification accuracy only; no transaction, commission or slippage terms appear in the text). OOS design: three expanding-window folds (train 2022, test 2023; train 2022 to 2023, test 2024; train 2022 to 2024, test 2025), hyperparameters "fixed before any walk-forward testing began", and a 200-shuffle permutation test on fold 3.
- Quality: single independent author, not peer reviewed, and the paper says it is third in a series of the author's own negative results. The small architecture was chosen on purpose. Four configurations and several target definitions were tried (Target A and B were rejected before training). This is the closest match in the log to the program's instrument class and horizon, but it is small and unreplicated.

### E1-ML-02. Zhang, Zohren, Roberts (2019), DeepLOB
- Citation: Zihao Zhang, Stefan Zohren, Stephen Roberts, "DeepLOB: Deep Convolutional Neural Networks for Limit Order Books", arXiv:1808.03668 v6. https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03668 (journal venue not verified from the fetched text).
- Retrieved: full text, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1808.03668.
- Claim: a CNN-plus-LSTM model on raw LOB data predicts mid-price direction over horizons of k = 20 to 100 LOB events. It transfers to stocks outside the training set. The trading simulation ignores costs.
- Quotes: "Overall, our LSE dataset is made up of 12 months, and has more than 134 million samples. On average, there are 150,000 events per day per stock. The events are irregularly spaced in time." / "∆k,k+1 is on average 0.192 seconds in the dataset. We take the first 6 months as training data, the next 3 months as validation data and the last 3 months as test data." / "For the purpose of the above simulation we use mid-prices without transaction costs. While in particular the second assumption is not a reasonable assumption for a standalone strategy, we argue that (i) it is enough for a relative comparison of the above models" / "Although we obtain worse accuracy for longer prediction horizons, the cumulative profits are actually higher as a more robust signal is generated." / "the proposed model generalises well to instruments that did not form part of the training data. This suggests the existence of universal features that are informative for price formation".
- Market: five liquid LSE stocks (transfer tests on five more), plus the FI-2010 benchmark. Horizon: k up to 100 events, which is about 20 s at 0.192 s per event (my arithmetic from the quoted mean; the paper does not state seconds per horizon). Data: 12 months, more than 134 million samples. Costs: none (mid-price fills, gross profits). OOS: chronological 6/3/3-month split.
- On longer horizons (the brief's question): the paper tests nothing beyond k = 100 events. Its only statement is the one quoted above, that accuracy falls as the horizon lengthens while simulated gross profit rises. It says nothing about minutes-to-hours horizons.
- Quality: Oxford-Man group, a widely cited anchor. Profits are gross, at mid-price, and one share per trade.

### E1-ML-03. Briola, Bartolucci, Aste (2024), Deep LOB forecasting
- Citation: Antonio Briola, Silvia Bartolucci, Tomaso Aste, "Deep Limit Order Book Forecasting: A microstructural guide", arXiv:2403.09267 v4 (June 2024). https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09267
- Retrieved: full text, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.09267.
- Claim: DeepLOB forecast quality depends on tick size, and good ML metrics do not mean the forecasts can be traded.
- Quotes: "We demonstrate that the stocks' microstructural characteristics influence the efficacy of deep learning methods and that their high forecasting power does not necessarily correspond to actionable trading signals. We argue that traditional machine learning metrics fail to adequately assess the quality of forecasts in the Limit Order Book context." / "we find that large-tick stocks are easier to predict, they are overall characterized by a stronger prediction signal, and their forecast performance is robust across prediction horizons. However, by examining the likelihood of observing a mid-price update over progressively shorter physical time intervals, we highlight that the utility of this signal is significantly dependent on the presence of low-latency hardware infrastructures."
- Market: 15 NASDAQ stocks, 2017 to 2019. Horizon: 10, 50 and 100 LOB updates. Costs: no net-of-cost P&L; the paper proposes a "complete transactions" practicality metric instead. OOS: train/validation/test splits per year (details not extracted).
- Quality: UCL group, open code (LOBFrame). A cautionary replication of DeepLOB. Horizons are far below the program's.

### E1-ML-04. Lucchese, Pakkanen, Veraart (2023), short-term predictability in order books
- Citation: Lorenzo Lucchese, Mikko S. Pakkanen, Almut E. D. Veraart, "The Short-Term Predictability of Returns in Order Book Markets: a Deep Learning Perspective", arXiv:2211.13777 v3 (Oct 2023). https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.13777 (publication venue not checked).
- Retrieved: full text, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13777.
- Claim: order-book predictability lasts up to about 50 to 300 order-book updates, which is milliseconds to about half a second. Pooled ("universal") models may learn different patterns from single-stock models.
- Quotes: "The predictability in price formation dynamics, already considered in the sense of next mid-price change in Sirignano and Cont (2019), was found to persist up to 50-300 order book updates ahead, horizons over which multiple mid-price changes may occur. Such predictable horizons might vary from a few milliseconds to nearly half a second, depending on the stock under consideration. These results contrast with low-frequency returns, where predictability is much harder to identify but easier to trade once discovered." / "in the high-frequency context, predictability is not always exploitable due to technological limitations and market microstructure issues." / "Our preliminary results suggest that deep learning architectures may pick up different predictable order book patterns when trained on a pool of stocks instead of a single one." / Data: "one year of open (9:30 EST) to close (16:00 EST) trading data for 115 Nasdaq tickers from January 2, 2019, to January 31, 2020. To produce results in feasible computational time, we select a subset of 10 stocks".
- Costs: none (predictability study). OOS: model confidence sets on held-out data.
- Quality: Imperial College, formal inference (Hansen model confidence sets). It is the direct evidence the brief asked for on how far LOB-driven deep-learning predictability extends in time: under a second.

### E1-ML-05. Pollok and Robik (2026), end-to-end policies on 16 CME futures
- Citation: Austin Pollok, Kevin Robik, "End-to-End Parametric Portfolio Policies for Cross-Asset Futures Timing: When Do AI Models Beat Simple Rules?", arXiv:2607.00475 (July 2026). https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00475
- Retrieved: full text, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.00475.
- Claim: on daily data for 16 liquid CME futures, a transformer policy and an LSTM policy trained on a differentiable Sharpe loss beat simple rules in some sleeves but not all. The pooled cross-asset transformer only matches 1/N. Costs separate the LSTM from the transformer.
- Quotes: "In gross terms, an LSTM and a transformer-based architecture perform comparably out-ofsample, but diverge when we consider transaction costs. The transformer generates the stronger learned policy, trades far less than the LSTM, and matches or exceeds equal weighting through moderate cost." / "the mean pairwise correlation is 0.67 within an asset class and 0.05 across, leaving the classes as near-orthogonal return processes." / "In those tests, however, the engineered features added little and transferred inconsistently beyond the raw return history itself." / "Under this correction, the equityindex results for both learned models remain significant, with t ≈ 3. The cross-asset transformer reaches t ≈ 2, which clears the uncorrected 5% level but not the Bonferroni-adjusted threshold." / "It assigns only probability 0.59 to the transformer outperforming equal weighting. The cross-asset transformer therefore matches, rather than significantly beats, 1/N ." / "Added complexity also does not automatically help. In our tests, seed averaging improves stability, but mixture-of-experts structures, larger feature sets, per-class tuning, and cross-model ensembling do not reliably improve out-of-sample performance." / Costs: "Following [11], we set λ to a realistic 2 basis points for these liquid contracts and also examine larger values".
- Market: ES, NQ, RTY, ZT, ZF, ZN, ZB, 6E, 6J, CL, NG, GC, HG, SI, ZC, ZW. Horizon: daily rebalancing, so not intraday. Data: daily bars, 2000/2001 to 2024, 16 contracts. Costs: 2 bp on turnover, plus a sensitivity sweep. OOS: expanding walk-forward, first 40% of the sample for training, then refits in roughly four-year blocks; the cross-asset OOS window is 2011 to 2024. The training recipe was tuned on the LSTM and "confirmed rather than researched" on the transformer.
- Quality: two authors (USC, plus a practitioner firm), not peer reviewed. It reports a Bonferroni correction across 7 universes and a stationary bootstrap. The daily horizon is outside the program's intraday range, but the universe is close to the program's product list.

### E1-ML-07. Aleti, Bollerslev, Siggaard, intraday market predictability from the factor zoo
- Citation: Saketh Aleti, Tim Bollerslev, Mathias Siggaard, "Intraday Market Return Predictability Culled from the Factor Zoo", manuscript MS-FIN-2023-01657.R1 submitted to Management Science. https://public.econ.duke.edu/~boller/Papers/HFML.pdf
- Retrieved: full text from the author's Duke page (not a fallback).
- Claim: 15-minute returns on the market are predictable out of sample from lagged 15-minute returns on 272 factor and industry portfolios. SPY trading on these forecasts survives half-spread costs. Several ML families were compared, and an equal-weight ensemble scored best.
- Quotes: "Using the predictions from our main forecasting model in the formulation of a simple trading strategy for the SPY ETF results in an annualized intraday transaction-cost-adjusted Sharpe ratio of 1.37, along with an annualized Fama-French six-factor risk-adjusted alpha of 20.83%." / "we follow Aleti (2023) and sample the returns at a “coarse” 15-minute frequency, for a total of 169,965 intraday return observations on each of the factors." / "The non-linear ML algorithms we consider are: Feedforward Neural Networks (FNN), Random Forests (RF), and Gradient Boosted Regression Trees (GBRT). Following common practice, we also consider an equally-weighted average of all of the above models sans OLS" / "The forecasts from Partial Least Squares (PLS), Feedforward Neural Networks (FFN), and Gradient Boosted Regression Trees (GRBT), in particular, when combined with the S-Sign strategy all achieve Sharpe ratios in excess of 1.20. ## Per-question synthesis (evidence only)

### Q1. Model families with OOS, net-of-cost predictability at 15 min to 1 day in liquid futures, and data sizes
- Direct evidence on CME futures at intraday horizons is thin. The only futures-intraday ML comparison in the log, E1-ML-01 (MNQ, 944 days of 5-minute bars, 10:30 to close), finds no OOS skill for gradient boosting or an LSTM (best permutation p = 0.135) and reports unstable feature importance across folds. It is single-author, unreviewed and cost-free.
- The only net-of-cost positive result at the program's 15-minute horizon is E1-ML-07. It covers the US equity market traded through SPY (not futures), uses a 1996 to 2020 sample and predictors built from 272 equity factor portfolios. Net Sharpe is 1.37 with a half-spread cost rule. PLS, FNN and GBRT all exceed a 1.20 Sharpe, the equal-weight ensemble is best, and the best OOS R² is 0.212%. It does not rank trees against networks.
- At daily horizons on liquid futures, deep sequence models trained on pooled cross-asset data report net-of-cost gains over TSMOM baselines: E1-ML-10 (88 futures, edge up to about 2 to 3 bp), E1-ML-11 (50 futures), E1-ML-12 (50 futures, cost term in the reward), E1-ML-14 (50 futures, 2010 to 2025, about 2x classical trend net). All come from one group (Oxford-Man) and compare against rule baselines, not against tree models. E1-ML-05 (16 CME futures, daily) is more mixed: learned policies lead in some sleeves, and the pooled transformer "matches, rather than significantly beats, 1/N".
- Trees versus deep networks: no source compares them on futures at 15 min to 1 day. E1-ML-08 (daily S&P 500 stocks, net of 5 bp per half-turn) ranks RF > GBT > DNN with an untuned DNN. E1-ML-06 (SPY, 1-minute) finds the LSTM helps and RF does not. E1-ML-09 (general tabular ML) finds trees best at about 10k samples, with the gap shrinking at 50k. The sources disagree by setting, and none settles the question for the program's setting.
- Order-book deep learning at longer horizons: DeepLOB (E1-ML-02) tests only up to 100 events (about 20 s from its 0.192 s mean spacing) with costless mid-price fills. Lucchese et al. (E1-ML-04) find predictability lasts about 50 to 300 updates, "a few milliseconds to nearly half a second". Briola et al. (E1-ML-03) find the signal's usefulness depends on low-latency infrastructure. No source shows LOB deep-learning predictability at minutes to hours. Kolm et al. (R01, "multiple horizons") could not be retrieved and is [unverified].
- Data-size statements: E1-ML-01's authors argue that transformer-scale models need "orders of magnitude" more than four years of one-instrument intraday data. E1-ML-05 reports that engineered features "added little" OOS. No source gives a quantitative sample-size threshold for futures intraday ML.

### Q2. Pooled cross-asset models: effective sample size and handling of cross-asset correlation
- Pooling is the default in the Oxford-Man futures work. E1-ML-10 trains on "all M possible prediction and target tuples across all N assets". Per-asset normalisation is by volatility scaling of targets and positions (E1-ML-10, E1-ML-12, E1-ML-13), and E1-ML-11 and E1-ML-14 add a learned per-asset embedding inside shared weights.
- Evidence that pooling beats per-asset models comes mostly from tick-level equities: Sirignano and Cont (E1-ML-15, about 1000 NASDAQ stocks) find a universal model beats asset-specific models, generalises to unseen stocks, and gains nothing from volatility/price/spread normalisation. Lucchese et al. (E1-ML-04) report, as preliminary, that pooled models may learn different patterns. At daily futures horizons, X-Trend (E1-ML-13) reports zero-shot transfer to held-out assets, and Poh et al. (E1-ML-16) report that parameter sharing or transfer reduces overfitting on short-history targets (crypto).
- Effective sample size: no source quantifies the effective-sample-size gain from pooling across correlated assets. The only explicit statement, in E1-ML-14, is N_eff ≈ B × L "up to dependence adjustments", and it concerns pooling time windows in the Sharpe loss, not assets.
- Correlation handling, as reported: X-Trend assumes a diagonal covariance (E1-ML-13), which the authors call reasonable "outside of the tail", and leaves out fixed-income contracts because they are more correlated. DeePM (E1-ML-14) regularises cross-asset dependence with a macro-graph prior and applies a strict lag to cross-asset attention because asynchronous closes create look-ahead. E1-ML-05 measures mean pairwise daily correlation of 0.67 within an asset class and 0.05 across classes on 16 CME futures. It uses Bonferroni across universes but makes no correlation adjustment to sample size. No source adjusts significance tests for cross-asset correlation within a pooled model.
- Disagreement: E1-ML-15 finds that standard normalisations do not help at tick level, while the futures papers treat volatility scaling as central (E1-ML-10 credits it among the key drivers). The settings differ (tick direction versus daily sizing).

### Q3. Leakage, purged/embargoed CV, CPCV, and how many configurations a search can try
- DSR (E1-ML-17): the expected maximum Sharpe grows with the number of independent trials. Reported Sharpes should be deflated by the trial count and its dispersion, with an effective N for correlated trials (Appendix 3). The authors call a backtest without search accounting "worthless".
- MinBTL (E1-ML-19): with 5 years of data, "no more than forty-five independent model configurations should be tried" before an in-sample SR of 1 is expected from noise. For 2 years the figure is 7. These counts assume independent trials, and the authors suggest PCA to estimate the effective N for dependent trials.
- PBO/CSCV (E1-ML-18): the selected configuration's out-of-sample rank against the median across all combinatorial splits. It needs every trial ("Hiding trials will lead to an underestimation"), and the authors say it may mislead when performance series are strongly autocorrelated and S is large. They call hold-out unreliable, especially for small samples.
- Search-count hurdles (E1-ML-22): the t-ratio hurdle for new factors rises from 2.0 to above 3.0 given the history of factor mining.
- Automated discovery (E1-ML-20): deflating by the agent's own trial ledger rejected every LLM-found strategy (up to 100 candidates). A leaky oracle with Sharpe 35 passed DSR and PBO, so these statistics do not detect look-ahead. The paper also reports that SR 0.6 needs about 11 years OOS to reach t ≈ 2, and that a low PBO did not stop an OOS collapse.
- Purging and embargo: no primary López de Prado text was retrieved (R04, blocked). The only full-text source is E1-ML-21, an applied use of CPCV with 21-day purge and embargo, N = 10 groups and k = 8 test groups (45 splits, 36 paths). The comparison of CPCV against walk-forward and K-fold (Arian et al., R03) is [unverified]. Its snippet claims CPCV had lower PBO and higher DSR.
- Cross-asset leakage: E1-ML-14 identifies same-day cross-asset mixing under asynchronous closes as a look-ahead that "disappears in live trading".

### Q4. Turning a fitted model into plain rules: what survives and how it is tested OOS
- General ML (labelled): RuleFit (E1-ML-25) fits lasso-selected short conjunctive rules drawn from tree ensembles and reports accuracy "comparable to the best methods", with interaction statistics built on partial dependence. Model-extraction trees (E1-ML-26) overfit unless new inputs are actively sampled and labelled by the black box. Fidelity is measured on held-out points as agreement with the black box, not with the true outcome. KDDT (E1-ML-29) targets structural stability of the distilled tree under data randomness and reports train and test accuracy close to the teacher (non-financial data).
- Explanation reliability (general ML): TreeSHAP (E1-ML-27) gives exact Shapley and interaction values for trees and shows path-based attributions are inconsistent. Molnar et al. (E1-ML-28) warn that PDP, PFI and SHAP mislead when the model overfits, when features are dependent (they extrapolate, and ALE is suggested instead), and when model-refit variance is ignored: an apparent effect "could cancel out once averaged over multiple model fits".
- Finance: on ES and B6 futures, E1-ML-23 checks that SHAP interactions agree with interactions read from CatBoost decision paths. That is an explanation-consistency test, not an out-of-sample test of a rule, and the underlying models beat no-information only in some configurations. E1-ML-24 (NASDAQ trade-level, one month) distils a DNN via Shapley analysis into a parametric model. On a 50/50 chronological split, the distilled model beats a linear VAR and comes "comparable" to the DNN in R². There is no trading test and no cost treatment.
- No source in the log takes a model trained on futures at 15 min to 1 day, distils it into rules, and tests the rules out of sample net of cost. For this program's setting: no source found.
- What the sources say survives distillation: short rules or low-order structure (RuleFit's claim; E1-ML-24's recent-lag nonlinear terms). What they say does not reliably survive: feature rankings under noise (E1-ML-01's fold-to-fold instability) and effects estimated on extrapolated or dependent feature regions (E1-ML-28).
